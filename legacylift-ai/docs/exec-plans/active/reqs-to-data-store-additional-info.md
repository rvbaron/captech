# Store generated requirements in a queryable data store — Additional Info (Archive)

This file is the `-additional-info` companion to the ExecPlan `reqs-to-data-store.md` in this same directory. Per `docs/exec-plan.md` ("Large plans: split current state from archived detail"), it holds detail that is no longer needed to understand or continue the *current* state of the work.

What it holds today is unusual for an archive, and worth stating plainly: **this plan was reviewed nine times before implementation began, and none of it has been built yet.** So the history here is not completed-milestone history — it is the per-finding record of those nine review rounds. One hundred and twenty-five findings were raised (`PR-01` through `PR-125`) and all one hundred and twenty-five are closed, meaning the main plan text was corrected in place and now reads as though each defect was never there. Each row below names the defect and what closed it.

**You do not need this file to continue current work.** The main plan file is self-contained for building every milestone. There is exactly one reason to open this archive: **before proposing a change to how the plan does something, check whether a review already considered and rejected it.** Many rows exist precisely to stop a tempting simplification being reintroduced — a shorter key, a merged column, a dropped defensive branch, a two-set assertion where three sets are needed — and each one records why the simpler thing fails. Reading a row costs a minute; rediscovering the defect it describes has cost this plan four rounds.

**The ninth round added a fourth reason, and with the eighth it is the pair to act on first.** Where the eighth round found rules that could not be built, the ninth found rules that were right and *unreachable* — a column four call sites assert and the schema lacks, a constant typed so the assertion mandated against it cannot pass, a value whose only stated producer the plan forbids to exist. Its findings cluster at **interfaces** rather than at rules, so the check that finds them is different: walk each value from where it is written to where it is read, and confirm the signature, the column and the constant on that path can carry it. Note also that `PR-119` corrects a closure `PR-112` wrote one round earlier, which is the third time a round has had to repair the previous round's own new assertion — treat an assertion added by a review as unverified until something computes it.

**The eighth round added a third reason, and it is still the one to act on first among the rules themselves.** Its three blockers were all rules that seven rounds of consistency-checking had passed over because they were internally coherent — and all three were caught the same way, by walking the rule through the concrete input it governs (SPEC-1 §S1.10's worked examples, `extractors.json`'s fifty-two entries, one re-ingest of one saved JSON). Before you build a step, do that for the rules it states.

**The sixth and seventh rounds added a second reason to open this file, and it applies while you are editing rather than before you start.** Those rounds found little new about the design and mostly defects about the *document*: a rule the fifth round corrected in its owning Step, with a stale copy of the same rule left standing elsewhere and still readable as an instruction. So when you change a rule in the main plan, read the sixth-round table below for the shape of that failure before you call the change done.

What remains *live* from these rounds stays in the main file under `Surprises & Discoveries`, not here: the recurring defect class and its six instances, the consolidated list of claims that four rounds have verified and that must not be re-checked, and the one earlier closure (`PR-22`) that a later one (`PR-48`) superseded, and the sixth round's stale-copy theme. Those change what an implementer should do next, so they are not archive material.

Two conventions used throughout, both worth knowing before reading a row:

- **Cite these `PR-` identifiers, not any numbering from the conversations that produced them.** That numbering was ephemeral and mapped to nothing in either document, which is why these identifiers exist.
- **A superseded closure is left standing rather than edited.** Where a later round replaced an earlier round's fix, both rows remain and the supersession is called out where it happened, so the sequence stays legible. `PR-22`/`PR-48`, `PR-01`/`PR-79` and `PR-70`/`PR-81` are the three instances.
- **A *completed* closure is a different thing from a superseded one, and the sixth round introduced it.** `PR-87` and `PR-91` do not replace `PR-79`; they close stale copies of a rule `PR-79` had already corrected in its owning Step. The distinction matters when reading a sequence: a supersession changes the answer, a completion changes only how many places carry it. **The seventh round found four more `PR-79` completions** — `PR-99`, `PR-100`, `PR-101` and `PR-103` — and one of them, `PR-99`, is a shape worth knowing on its own: `PR-79` deleted a *step* rather than a rule, and the column that step existed to fill (`implementation_notes`) was left in the schema with no writer. When a finding removes a step, check what that step was the only producer of.

**A closing section, `What the nine rounds cost and bought, measured`, answers a different question from the rest of this file: whether another review round is worth running.** It is the evidence behind the instruction in the main plan's `Surprises & Discoveries` to stop reviewing and build Milestone 0. Read it if you are about to propose a tenth round, or if you want to know why the ninth was the last.

As milestones complete, their `Concrete Steps` and full `Outcomes & Retrospective` entries belong here too, alongside these tables. This archive is self-contained for the history it records.

## What the nine rounds cost and bought, measured

Written after round 9, in answer to a direct question from the plan's owner: *are these rounds still
finding new things, or is the plan churning?* The answer decides whether a tenth round is worth
running, so it is recorded here with the numbers rather than left as an impression. The conclusion
it leads to is live rather than historical and sits in the main file under `Surprises &
Discoveries`; this section is its evidence.

**Method.** Every finding row in this archive was counted per round, and each was checked for
whether it cites a `PR-` identifier from an *earlier* round. A finding that cites an earlier one is
either correcting that closure, completing it, or working inside the concept it established — it is
rework, in the broad sense. A finding that cites none is fresh ground.

**There is a regime change at R4/R5 and it is not subtle.** Rounds 1–3 produced 63 findings of which
one referred to earlier work. Rounds 5–9 produced 47 of which 24 did — a little over half. The
reviews stopped exploring the design around round 4 and have since been largely working over ground
the earlier rounds opened.

### The churn is in the specification, not in the requirements

The distinction matters because only one of the two is a reason for concern.

**Requirements churn — one instance in 125 findings.** `PR-79` moved SPEC-1 notation authorship from
ingest to the extractor, which changed what the system does rather than how the plan says it. It was
driven by measurement against the 470-rule reference corpus — 66% of `then` values passive, 51%
carrying a semicolon, 36% an `and`, `[Formula]` and `[Value]` with no source field at all — and not
by anyone re-arguing a settled point. Its propagation cost is legible: `PR-87` and `PR-91` (R6),
`PR-99`–`PR-101` and `PR-103` (R7), `PR-113` (R8). One decision, seven follow-on findings, three
rounds. That is the price of a correct reversal, not evidence of instability.

**Nothing else has reversed.** The load-bearing decisions have not been reopened once in nine
rounds: the store in `knowledge.sqlite`, merge rather than truncate-and-insert, no migration of the
old corpora, `gr_id` never content-derived, `anchor_key` additive with `symbols.id` untouched, the
under-merge bias with no auto-merge at any confidence, the shadow-column mechanism, SPEC-1 as
normative, and Milestone 0 first. `Purpose / Big Picture` is unchanged since it was written.

**Specification churn — substantial, and concentrated in three artifacts.** In each, the requirement
held still while its written expression was wrong repeatedly:

- **Field ownership**, seven rounds and still being corrected in R9: `PR-45` → `PR-70` → `PR-81` →
  `PR-86`/`PR-92` → `PR-99` → `PR-112` → `PR-119`. The requirement never moved — protect human
  edits, catch a new column — while the expression went two-set, three-set, four-set, then mistyped
  so its own assertion could not pass.
- **The slot split**: `PR-28` → `PR-107` → `PR-117`, `PR-123`.
- **`entity_class`**: `PR-83` → `PR-90` → `PR-105` → `PR-108`.

### Three rounds in a row repaired the previous round's own new assertion

`PR-70`'s acceptance criterion was false on day one and `PR-81` replaced it. `PR-99`'s was false when
written and `PR-112` replaced it. `PR-112`'s replacement was typed so that it could never pass and
`PR-119` replaced that. Three of the last twenty-seven findings exist only because a review round
created them.

This is the review process's own failure mode and it has a name in this archive already, added by R8:
**an assertion added by a review round is not thereby verified.** An assertion written in prose is
not executed by anything, so a review that adds them is manufacturing work for the next review at a
measurable rate. It is the strongest single argument for converting the assertions into tests rather
than into more prose.

### The find rate is not converging

25, 18, 20, 15, 7, 13, 8, 7, 12. It has sat between 7 and 13 for five rounds, and R9 produced **six**
blockers against R8's three. There is no trend here that reaches zero, because each round that
changes its question opens a seam the previous question could not see — R8 asked whether a rule could
be *built*, R9 asked whether a value could travel from its writer to its reader, and each found a
fresh class immediately. That is a property of reviewing a 1,700-line specification for a system that
does not exist yet: the reviews are simulating an implementer, and a simulation finds defects in
proportion to how faithfully it simulates.

### What the last round's findings say about where the remaining defects live

All six of R9's blockers were interface-level: a column four call sites assert and the schema lacks
(`PR-114`); a constant typed so its mandated assertion cannot pass (`PR-119`); a value whose only
stated producer the plan forbids to exist (`PR-115`); a check specified as a database lookup inside a
signature holding no database (`PR-116`); an undefined branch (`PR-117`); two sections issuing
contradictory instructions about one file (`PR-118`).

**Every one of those would surface in the first hour of writing the step it belongs to** — most from
a type checker or the first `pytest` run, and `PR-117` from the §S1.10 test the plan already
mandates. Reviewing 1,700 lines of prose to find them costs a full pass; the toolchain finds them for
free.

**`PR-124` is the honest counterexample and it is what review genuinely buys.** The reserved
`excluded` domain becoming eligible as a business subject would not have failed any build. It would
have shipped and quietly produced a corpus whose rules have "excluded" as their subject — a silent
wrong output, discovered late, after the model time that produced the corpus was already spent. The
same is true of R5's corpus measurements and `PR-83`'s collision count. That is the vein worth
mining, and in R9 it was one finding in twelve.

### Conclusion

Rounds 1–5 bought a great deal, including one genuine and correct reversal. Rounds 6–7 were largely
the blast radius of that reversal. Rounds 8–9 changed the question and found real blockers, but of a
kind that implementation surfaces faster and cheaper than reading does.

**A tenth round of the same kind is not worth running.** What is worth running is Milestone 0, which
has been untouched by five consecutive rounds — longer than anything else in this plan — and depends
on none of the three churned artifacts. See `Surprises & Discoveries` in the main file for the
instruction and for the two conditions that *would* justify another round.
